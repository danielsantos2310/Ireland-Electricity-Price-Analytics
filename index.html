Ireland’s electricity prices changed regime.

Business electricity was broadly stable for years. Then the 2021–2022 energy crisis rewrote the curve. This story follows the shock, who paid what, what forecasting could—and could not—learn, and three transparent paths to 2035.

01 / The break

For years, prices barely moved. Then the market broke upward.

From 2015 through 2020, the annual business price sat in a narrow range around 12–14 c/kWh. The crisis arrived quickly: gas and wholesale electricity surged, and retail prices followed.

02 / Wholesale shock

The spot market explains the crisis. It does not automatically predict retail.

SEMOpx’s annual Day-Ahead Market average jumped to €225.57/MWh in 2022, then fell sharply. Retail prices eased more slowly because retail pricing also reflects procurement, hedging, networks, taxes and contract timing.

04 / Prediction reality

The simplest forecast was the hardest to beat.

Rolling-origin validation from 2018 S1 to 2025 S2 tested trend, Holt and gas-augmented models against a persistence baseline. Complexity did not win.

Best RMSE2.43c/kWh

Naive: next semester ≈ last observed price.

Lagged wholesale features were useful explanatory variables, but still failed to beat the naive benchmark on the crisis-period walk-forward test.

06 / What it means

Energy prices are not one variable. They are a system.

01

Wholesale shocks matter.

They shaped the 2021–2023 crisis, but retail adjusts through contracts, hedging and other cost layers.

02

Infrastructure matters too.

Network investment can push one component upward even as renewable penetration changes energy costs.

03

Customer scale changes exposure.

The public Band IG benchmark is materially below the weighted business average.

04

Uncertainty belongs in the output.

The honest 2035 answer is a range of transparent scenarios, not a false-precision point estimate.
